The library

The internet democratized content. It never democratized curriculum.

Anyone on Earth can watch a lecture from MIT, read a physics paper, or find ten thousand videos on growing tomatoes. And yet learning anything real still feels like standing in a library where every book has been torn from its shelf and scattered on the floor.

That's because access to content was never the hard part. The hard part is sequence — knowing what to learn first, what to skip, what order unlocks what, and how to tell when you've actually learned it. That sequencing layer is what universities really sell. It's what a good mentor gives you. It's what an algorithm will never give you, because an algorithm is optimized for your attention, not your growth.

We haven't evolved much past books and universities. Not because the knowledge isn't out there — because the maps aren't.

We're building the maps.

What this is

A worldwide, community-built system of learning maps: step-by-step paths for learning things — for joy, for skill, for a richer life. How to read the night sky. How to bring a garden through its first season. How to get a language back that you used to speak. How to practice compassion, deliberately, for ninety days.

Think of it the way Wikipedia relates to knowledge. Wikipedia is the world's reference — a place to look things up. This is the world's curriculum — a place to look up how to learn things, built and maintained by the people who learned them.

Every map is:

A path, not a pile. Ordered steps with a clear progression, not a link dump. You always know where you are and what's next.

Yours when you start it. Start any map and your copy tracks your progress — your checkmarks, your notes, your pace. A map you're walking is a different thing from a map you're reading.

Made of resources anyone on Earth can open. Our one hard rule: every resource on every map must be freely and legally accessible to everyone. No paywalls, no "just buy the textbook," no region locks. A gold-standard video, an open-access paper, a public-domain classic, a free tool — if the whole world can't open it, it doesn't go on the map.

Kept alive by people. Links die. Videos get taken down. The best resource in 2026 is stale by 2029. Every map has a community behind it whose job includes replacing what breaks with the next-best thing that exists. No curated list on the internet has ever survived this; ours are built to.

Curated by humans. On purpose.

Every previous answer to "what should I learn next?" has been an algorithm — a feed hoping to bless you with something relevant between the things it's paid to show you. Feeds are why you can scroll for three hours and come away with nothing.

There is no feed here. There is no infinite scroll. When you open this, you see the paths you are walking and where you left off. The front page of this platform is your own life.

The maps themselves are made the way the best open-source software is made — not by vote, and not by committee:

Every map has a named maintainer. A real person with real knowledge of the subject, whose name is on the work. They set the standard and have final say.

Anyone can contribute. Found a better video? A broken link? A step that should come earlier? Propose the change. The maintainer reviews and merges. Many walk the path; some tend it; one person owns it.

This is how the most-loved educational resource in software — a community project with millions of users — actually works. It is not a democracy of edits. It is a commons with gardeners.

"Hasn't this been tried?"

Yes. Wikiversity launched in 2006 with almost this exact mission. Wikibooks too. They became ghost towns, and it's worth being honest about why — because the fix is the whole design of this project.

They failed on product: a wiki page about learning guitar is still just a page. There was nothing to fork, nothing to track, no state, no "you are here." Reading about a path is not walking one.

And they failed on governance: Wikipedia's neutral-consensus editing is the right model for facts and the wrong model for teaching. There is no neutral point of view on how to learn something — good curriculum is opinionated. Consensus editing sanded every path down to mush.

Trackable, forkable maps solve the first. Named maintainers solve the second. The mission was never wrong. The tools were.

What this costs you

Nothing. Every map, every resource, every contribution — free, forever, for everyone. That isn't a promotional period. It's the point.

The project sustains itself the way healthy open projects do: the maps are free, and optional conveniences on top of them — synced notes, an AI study companion that knows where you are on your path, backups and extras — support the work for those who want them and can pay. If you never pay a cent, you get the entire library. If you do, you're keeping it open for someone who can't.
